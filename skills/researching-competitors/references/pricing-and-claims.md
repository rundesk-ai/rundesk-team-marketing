# Prices you can see, and claims anyone can make

## List price is not price, on the accounting standard's own terms

The revenue-recognition standard states that a contractually stated price or a list price for a good
or service **may be (but shall not be presumed to be)** the standalone selling price of that good or
service. That is the authoritative answer to "their pricing page says $79".

Vendors estimate the standalone selling price using historical sales data **including discounts from
list price**, aggregated by geography and selling method to produce a median or average. Auditors have
treated that estimate as a critical audit matter and tested the mathematical accuracy of the median
discount off list — which tells you the discount is large enough, and variable enough, to be a matter
of significant judgment in an audit.

**What a pricing page establishes:** the published offer, the packaging, the metering unit, what is
gated to which tier, and what requires a conversation. All genuinely useful, all about intent.
**What it does not establish:** what any customer paid.

Where a rival's filings disclose that revenue changed materially, the management discussion must
describe how much of the change came from **prices** versus **volume**. That is the only routinely
available public statement about realized pricing direction, and it is worth more than the pricing
page.

## Published pricing structures change faster than analyses do

Academic modelling of SaaS pricing found the configuration space grows **exponentially with the
number of add-ons** and linearly with the number of plans, and that the frequency of change within
pricing elements continues to increase. The same body of work, testing automated analysis across more
than 150 pricing models, **identified errors in 35 of them** — vendors publish pricing pages that do
not internally cohere.

Two rules follow:

- **Date every pricing observation and name the tier and metering unit.** An undated pricing
  comparison is worthless within a quarter.
- **Do not treat a pricing page as internally consistent.** If a tier's stated limits contradict its
  feature list, that is a finding about the page, not a fact about the product.

## Comparative claims: the actual standard

Policy in this area **encourages** naming or referring to competitors, while requiring clarity and,
where necessary, disclosure to avoid deceiving the consumer. Comparative advertising, when truthful
and nondeceptive, is described as a source of important information that assists consumers in making
rational purchase decisions, and as something that encourages product improvement and can lower
prices.

Disparaging advertising is **permissible so long as it is truthful and not deceptive** — a seller may
honestly inform the public of its product's advantages over competing products even though the
comparison has the effect of disparaging them.

**The bar is the same, not higher.** Industry codes and interpretations imposing a higher standard of
substantiation on comparative claims than on unilateral ones are treated as inappropriate. The
governing standard is the ordinary one: an advertiser needs a **reasonable basis** for a claim before
it is disseminated, and where the claim expressly references a level of support — "tests prove",
"studies show" — at least that advertised level is expected.

Comparative advertising in the policy sense means advertising that **compares alternative brands on
objectively measurable attributes or price, and identifies the alternative brand** by name,
illustration, or other distinctive information. If your comparison is not on objectively measurable
attributes, it is not this kind of claim and cannot be substantiated as one.

## Superlatives are claims, not puffery

The self-regulatory position on "#1" is direct: such claims are **not** classified as puffery, so they
carry a heavier substantiation burden than a boastful opinion would. The reasoning is worth
internalizing — whether a product is best is a matter of opinion, but **how many people hold that
opinion is provable.**

The governing precedent for a software "#1" claim built on review data set two things:

- To support a "Rated #1" claim within a broad product category, an advertiser should **as a general
  rule compare itself to at least 85% of the relevant marketplace.**
- A review platform's collection of user reviews **did not provide reliable evidence** to support the
  claim or demonstrate comparative user satisfaction, and there was insufficient information about
  how the platform created its rankings for the methodology to be evaluated.

A related decision on star ratings makes the limitation explicit: ratings **do not indicate why the
rating was given**, and may have been given for reasons other than the attribute being advertised.

And on implied breadth: a recent decision found that claims about a software assistant conveyed the
message that it worked seamlessly with all user files with no material limitations on file type,
size, length, or number — and required some claims be modified or discontinued. **Implied capability
breadth is itself a claim requiring support.**

## What this means for your analysis

- **Never repeat a competitor's superlative as a fact.** Write "they claim X" and state whether a
  basis was published. If their "#1" rests on a review platform, the precedent above says that is
  probably not substantiation.
- **Never let a testimonial stand in for evidence.** Statements from satisfied customers are not
  sufficient to support a claim requiring objective evaluation.
- **Apply the same standard to your own side.** A comparison you produce that names a rival on
  objectively measurable attributes is a comparative claim, and it needs a reasonable basis before it
  goes out. This skill produces the evidence; whoever publishes it owns the substantiation.
- **Flag the legal exposure without giving a legal opinion.** An unsubstantiated superlative or an
  unsupported implied-breadth claim is a real risk, and naming it is part of an honest competitive
  analysis. Naming the applicable rule is not the same as advising on it — route that to counsel.
