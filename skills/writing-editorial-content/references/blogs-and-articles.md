# Write blogs and articles

A blog is a channel; an article is a form. Establish both the editorial job and the publishing
context before choosing a structure. This method applies to original posts, reported or explanatory
articles, tutorials, analysis, case studies, announcements, and substantial revisions.

## Turn the request into an assignment

Write a one-paragraph assignment before a long draft:

```text
For <specific reader in a specific situation>, establish <one useful point or change>.
Use <firsthand evidence, reporting, product facts, examples, or analysis>.
The reader should leave able to <understand, decide, or do something supported>.
The piece belongs on <publication/channel> because <publisher authority or contribution>.
Do not imply <nearest unsupported claim, state, or promise>.
```

Name the form inside the channel. An announcement leads with established news and its consequence;
an explanation makes a mechanism intelligible; a tutorial produces a verifiable outcome; analysis
interprets evidence and limits; a case study proves only the supplied case; a reported feature
earns breadth through sources; an opinion article makes the author's stance visible. Do not turn
every subject into a generic listicle or disguise product promotion as independent reporting.

If the assignment is not yet supportable, return a content brief or reporting plan rather than a
full article padded with background. A useful brief records the reader, question, central point,
fresh contribution, source plan, claim risks, likely structure, publication needs, and acceptance
check. It is a research map, not a prewritten conclusion that reporting must confirm.

## Report before drafting

Inventory what the piece needs and what the supplied material actually establishes:

```text
Question or section | evidence needed | available source | missing reporting | claim limit
```

Prefer primary documents, direct observations, accountable subject-matter sources, and original
data appropriate to the claim. Use secondary sources for context, scrutiny, or synthesis rather
than laundering their reporting as the author's own. Seek source variety by knowledge and
relationship to the subject, not an arbitrary quotation count. A product post can be authoritative
without outside quotations when it accurately explains the publisher's own work; an industry claim
cannot borrow that authority.

For interviews, prepare open questions tied to the assignment, ask for concrete examples and
limits, verify names and technical terms, distinguish on-record language from background, and check
every quotation against the record. Never write the desired quotation first or smooth a speaker's
meaning into a stronger claim.

## Build the reader's path

Choose an architecture that answers the reader's next important question. Before prose, make a
section map in which each part has a job and evidence:

```text
Opening promise -> necessary context -> development or proof -> complication or limit -> earned ending
```

This is a diagnostic, not a mandatory five-section template. Tutorials may follow prerequisites,
steps, verification, and recovery. Announcements may use news, relevance, detail, availability, and
next action. Reported features may braid scene and context. Explanations may move from observed
effect to mechanism and consequence. Use chronology only when sequence carries meaning.

The opening must orient or create useful tension without hiding the subject. Establish the focus
and significance early enough that the reader understands the promise; a visible `nut graf` is one
option, not a quota. Every section must advance, substantiate, complicate, or resolve that promise.
Put qualifications beside the claims they bound. End when the reader's question is answered or
productively reframed; do not append a generic summary or an unearned sales pitch.

## Draft the whole article system

Write the body before finalizing its packaging. Then create only the elements the channel needs:

- **Title:** a specific, accurate promise whose ordinary reading the evidence supports.
- **Dek or standfirst:** added context or stakes, not a duplicate title or unsupported tease.
- **Excerpt or summary:** a self-contained account of what the piece gives the reader.
- **Byline and author note:** the correct accountable author and relevant authority, never invented.
- **Headings:** meaningful navigation and information scent, not keyword or frequency quotas.
- **Links and citations:** descriptive, proximate to the supported claim, and appropriate to house style.
- **Media notes:** what an image, diagram, or example must communicate; do not invent an asset's content.
- **Call to action:** the next step the piece earns and the owner has approved, not a default conversion ask.

Write titles after the focus is stable and offer variants only when useful criteria distinguish
them. Do not claim a headline, length, keyword density, publishing cadence, or format is `best`
without current evidence for the specific publication and audience. Search intent can clarify a
reader need; it cannot substitute for original value or authorize search-engine filler.

## Revise at article scale

First perform the package's truth, substance, and structure passes. Then inspect:

1. whether the title, opening, section sequence, and ending fulfill the same promise;
2. whether every major section contains evidence, explanation, example, or necessary reasoning;
3. whether context arrives before readers need it and background has displaced no original value;
4. whether quotations add knowledge or texture rather than repeat the narrator;
5. whether transitions express the actual logical relationship;
6. whether paragraphs vary with thought and remain usable on the intended screen; and
7. whether metadata, links, media requirements, disclosures, and calls to action are accurate.

For an update or correction, preserve the publication's change history when required, identify what
changed and why, and do not silently make a materially different claim appear to have been the
original. For a review, distinguish a fact defect, reporting gap, structural issue, line-level
problem, and house-style choice so the owner can act on the right layer.

## Accept or stop

A finished blog post or article must pass these questions:

- Can a named reader tell quickly what this is about and why it is worth their time?
- Does the publisher or author contribute knowledge, reporting, experience, or judgment rather than
  merely restating familiar material?
- Can every factual, attributed, comparative, experiential, and product-state claim be traced?
- Does the form match the evidence and clearly distinguish reporting, interpretation, and promotion?
- Does the structure carry one coherent promise without padding or template residue?
- Does the language sound like the approved author or house while remaining clear to the audience?
- Are the title, excerpt, links, media notes, disclosure, and next action as truthful as the body?

If not, revise, shorten, change form, or return the missing reporting. A polished shell is not a
finished article.
