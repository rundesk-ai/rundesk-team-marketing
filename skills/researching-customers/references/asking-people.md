# Asking people directly

Read this before designing a survey, writing an interview guide, or recruiting anyone.

## Authority and the research boundary

Contacting people is an external effect. It needs explicit authority, and the professional standard
draws one line harder than any other: **research must stay separate from selling.**

The requirements a defensible program meets:

- Data subjects are informed of any **non-research purpose** — promotional, commercial, or customer
  experience follow-up — **before data collection begins**, and **separate consent is obtained** for
  those purposes.
- The researcher identifies themselves promptly and verifiably.
- Participation is voluntary, based on clear and accurate information about the purpose and nature of
  the research, provided at the start.
- Participants are told if there will be re-contact, and agree to it.
- Retention is stated: how long personal data is kept, and for what.
- Participants may withdraw at any time, and on withdrawal their personal data is deleted where that
  is technically and operationally feasible.
- Nothing is disclosed that could identify a participant with their data without permission.

The same standard prohibits conducting sales, fundraising, or campaigning **under the guise of
research**, and requires no false or misleading claims about a study's sponsorship or purpose.

Where a generated persona rather than a person conducts the interaction, current guidance requires
that the data subject be told at the beginning.

**Also settle the lawful basis for using a customer list at all.** Reusing data collected for service
delivery to recruit for research is a purpose change, and consent is only one of several possible
bases. This is a question for whoever owns privacy compliance, not something to assume.

For human-subjects research at covered institutions, the relevant exemption covers interview and
survey procedures where the information is recorded without readily ascertainable identity, or where
disclosure would not reasonably place subjects at risk of liability or damage to financial standing,
employability, educational advancement, or reputation. Ordinary internal product research is usually
outside that regulatory scope — but **those criteria are the right structural test** for whether an
interview program is low-risk, whoever is running it.

## Survey design

**Report the instrument, not just the result.** Professional disclosure practice requires, at minimum:
sponsorship, the exact question wording, the population, how the sample was generated, the mode, the
dates, sample sizes, weighting, and a limitations statement. A result published without these is not
independently reviewable, which is the entire point of publishing it.

**State the sample type explicitly** — whether it came from a probability-based frame or from opt-in,
volunteer, or other non-probability sources. Precision measures for a non-probability sample may only
be given alongside a description of how the model was specified, its assumptions validated, and the
measures calculated.

**If you report a response rate, compute it by the standard definitions**, which give a family of
formulas from a minimum to a maximum rate. Where the rate requires estimating what share of
unknown-eligibility cases were eligible, that estimate must be guided by the best available
information — and explicitly **must not be selected to boost the response rate.** Note also that a
response rate alone does not establish how much nonresponse error exists, or whether it exists.

**Assume every survey carries unmeasured error and say so.** The professional code requires a general
statement acknowledging it.

### The wording traps, with measured effects

- **Adding a consequence changes support.** 68% versus 43% on the same underlying question when one
  version named potential casualties.
- **Closed options manufacture salience.** 58% chose "the economy" from a list; 35% volunteered it.
  43% gave an unlisted answer in the open format against 8% in the closed one — so a closed list
  hides most of the distribution.
- **Order effects run both ways.** A preceding item can create contrast (45% versus 37%) or
  assimilation (81% versus 66%).
- **Logically equivalent options are not equivalent.** Respondents endorse "should not be allowed"
  more readily than "should be forbidden".
- **Acquiescence** — agreeing with a statement as presented — is stronger among less educated and
  less informed respondents.
- **Social desirability** — respondents understate alcohol and drug use, tax evasion, and racial
  bias, and overstate church attendance, charitable giving, and likelihood of voting. Misreporting on
  sensitive topics is intentional and design-sensitive, not random noise.

A published taxonomy of response bias defines the general case: response bias exists when reported
values systematically deviate from the true values, often because respondents are minimizing
cognitive effort rather than lying.

### The instrument that is not research

A short instrument whose items are uniformly negative about one option, where the sponsor is unnamed
or disguised, is a persuasion tool rather than a measurement — the polling profession names this
explicitly and calls it unethical. The same standard refuses the lazy version of that critique:
containing negative information about an option does not by itself make an instrument illegitimate.
Judge it on whether it is built to measure or to move.

### Panel quality

Non-probability online opt-in samples averaged **about twice the error** of probability-based panels
in a direct comparison, with the worst errors in the subgroups most often reported on — roughly 11
percentage points of error for young adults and for Hispanic adults, against under 4 points for
probability-based panels.

A meaningful share of that error is **bogus respondents** — 4% to 7% depending on the source, against
trace levels in address-recruited panels — who make little or no effort to answer truthfully and who
**tend to select positive answer choices**, so they bias results in a direction rather than adding
noise.

And the finding that kills the usual reassurance: **a representative demographic profile does not
predict accuracy.** Widespread errors were found on estimates for Black and Hispanic respondents even
in demographically matched samples.

## Interviews

- **Ask about the last time, not about preferences.** Recall of a specific episode is evidence;
  prediction of future behaviour is not.
- **Do not describe your solution before asking about the problem.** Once named, it contaminates
  everything after it.
- **Record the guide and the deviations.** A semi-structured interview is only reproducible if the
  starting instrument is written down.
- **Sample deliberately.** Who you did not talk to determines what you cannot conclude — most often
  the people who left, who never started, and who chose a competitor.

### How many is enough

Empirical tests of saturation found it reached within **9 to 17 interviews** or **4 to 8 focus
groups** — and specifically in **homogeneous populations with narrowly defined objectives**. Larger
samples were needed for multi-country work and for saturation of meaning rather than of codes.

Open work on the same question found roughly 6 interviews captured about 80% of themes in homogeneous
samples, 8 to 9 in more diverse ones, and 11 to 14 at a conservative threshold of no new information.
It also notes that saturation methods largely address sample size **before** collection and provide
little for judging adequacy during or after it.

A stronger frame than counting is **information power**: the more relevant information the sample
holds, the fewer participants are needed, with adequacy depending on the aim of the study, sample
specificity, use of established theory, quality of dialogue, and analysis strategy. Prefer this to a
target number, and state which of the five you are relying on.

## What to report

The question text, the mode, how people were recruited and from where, how many were approached and
how many answered, the dates, who sponsored it, what the sample cannot support, and the raw
distribution before any interpretation. Anything less is a claim about people that nobody can check.
