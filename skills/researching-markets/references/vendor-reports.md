# Published market figures and why they usually fail

Read this before citing any "market to reach $X billion by 20XX" number, including one a client or
executive already believes.

## The pathology, with a worked case

The figure **$1,811.75 billion by 2030** — six significant figures — was published for the global
artificial-intelligence market by one research firm in July 2023 at a compound annual growth rate of
37.3%, and by an unrelated firm in May 2025, marketed as an exclusive report, at 36.6%.

**The mismatched growth rate is the tell.** Two different rates cannot both produce the identical
terminal figure to the cent from a different base year. The endpoint was taken, not derived. The same
string then appears across finance and trade outlets, each carrying a press release, each looking
like a separate source.

Meanwhile another firm put the same nominal market at **$467 billion** for the same year — a spread
of nearly four times. Part of that gap is real: one scope is AI software and the other is AI broadly.
**That is the lesson.** Most headline divergence is undisclosed scope divergence, and neither release
states its boundary well enough to reconcile. When you cannot reconcile two figures, you do not have
two estimates; you have two assertions.

## How a number outlives its own retraction

The claim that there would be 50 billion connected devices by 2020 originated in a vendor CEO's 2010
shareholder presentation and was repeated the following year in a white paper by an employee of a
different vendor. Both originators later cut their own numbers materially. Contemporaries diverged
widely for the same year. The original figure continued to be cited anyway, long after the people who
made it up had walked it back.

**A figure's survival in the literature says nothing about its accuracy.** Trace it to the first
person who said it, then check whether they still believe it.

## Provenance can end in nothing

A government audit of widely cited counterfeiting loss estimates found that three of them could not
be substantiated because the underlying studies did not exist. On individual figures the auditors
reported that one agency had no record of the source data or methodology and could not corroborate
its own press-release number; that another agency's figures were of uncertain origin, had been
discredited, and were the subject of an internal memo telling staff to stop using them — while a
different part of the same department kept using them; and that a third agency could not locate any
record of an estimate widely attributed to it.

The audit also traced a laundering path three hops long: a trade association attributed a figure to a
federal agency, and an international body then cited the association report. The chain ended in a
void, and every link looked like a citation.

**Institutional attribution is not provenance.** "According to the FTC" is a claim about a document,
and the document may not exist.

## What to do instead

- **Open the method or do not cite the number.** If the report is paywalled and the press release
  states no method, population, or boundary, you have an assertion. Say "asserted by X, method not
  published" or leave it out.
- **Count a press release once.** Syndication is not corroboration. Trace every copy to its origin
  and record one source in the ledger.
- **Check whether the growth rate reproduces the endpoint.** It takes one division and it catches
  fabrication.
- **Compare boundaries before comparing numbers.** Two figures for "the same market" that differ by
  more than a factor of two are almost always measuring different things.
- **Name the interest.** A vendor sizing a market it sells into, a company sizing its own addressable
  market, and a platform indexing its own activity are all interested parties. Interest does not
  disqualify a source; concealing it disqualifies your analysis.
- **Prefer a defensible range you built to a precise number someone else printed.**

## What no source establishes

There is no academic paper, regulatory finding, or investigative work auditing the syndicated
market-report industry as a whole. The evidence above is specific and verifiable but it is about
individual figures, not a demonstrated industry-wide failure rate. State the limit that way. The
honest position is that these figures are of unknown provenance and must be treated as such, not
that they are known to be false.
