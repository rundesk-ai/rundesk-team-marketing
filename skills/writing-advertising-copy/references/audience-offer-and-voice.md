# Align audience, offer, attraction, and voice

## Find the qualified audience moment

Do not write for a demographic label alone. Record what the person is trying to decide or do, what
they already know, which alternatives they recognize, what matters to them, and what would make the
next action reasonable. Use approved customer research, search-query evidence, sales or support
language, first-party behavior, or owner direction. Do not invent a pain point because the product
appears capable of solving it.

Separate:

- **need or desire:** what progress the audience values;
- **offer:** what is actually available on stated terms;
- **benefit:** what relevant value the approved product direction or evidence supports;
- **differentiator:** why this option differs from the considered alternatives;
- **proof:** what makes the benefit or difference credible; and
- **action:** what the destination lets the person do now.

A feature is not automatically a benefit, and a benefit is not automatically proved. Translate a
feature only through approved product purpose or evidence. If differentiation is absent, use clear
category relevance and offer terms instead of inventing uniqueness.

## Choose an attraction angle

Use the narrowest strong supported angle:

| Angle | Requires | Failure to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Problem recognition | established audience problem and product relevance | diagnosing or shaming the reader |
| Desired outcome | approved benefit and realistic scope | promising a result the evidence cannot guarantee |
| Capability | concrete feature and useful context | feature lists with no reason to care |
| Offer | exact price, term, promotion, availability, and destination | false urgency or hidden conditions |
| Proof | attributable evidence appropriate to the claim | vague authority, cherry-picked or atypical results |
| Comparison | fixed alternative, criterion, evidence, and approval | undefined `better`, disparagement, or moving criteria |
| Identity or values | approved brand stance and audience relevance | identity pressure or pretending consensus |

The ad should answer, in the surface's available space: `Is this for me? What is it? Why might I
care? Why believe it? What happens next?` It need not print five separate sentences. Omit a layer
only when the creative, brand recognition, or destination context already carries it.

## Preserve a compelling voice

Make a compact voice card:

```text
Point of view and stance toward the audience:
Words the brand uses and avoids:
Syntax, rhythm, and degree of directness:
Formality, warmth, humor, and emotional range:
How confidently it makes supported claims:
How the voice asks for action:
```

Compression reveals weak voice work: many drafts collapse into `Unlock`, `Transform`, `Discover`,
or `Game-changing`. Replace generic promotion with the brand's actual relationship, specific offer,
and concrete value. Voice is not decorative; it determines who feels addressed and what kind of
promise the advertiser appears to make.

Preserve voice across variants while changing the persuasive hypothesis. A brand does not become a
different character merely because one treatment leads with the problem and another with proof.
Never paste a memorable voice-sample line without explicit reuse authority. Infer how the sample
speaks, not what the new ad may claim: a sample about making state `legible` does not authorize
`legible` as the product value, just as a sample about quiet launches does not establish reliability.
