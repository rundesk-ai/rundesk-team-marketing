# Review platforms: who owns them, how they are filled, what they prove

## Four of the five are now one company

**G2 acquired Capterra, Software Advice, and GetApp**, completing in early February 2026 for
approximately $110 million. The seller's own annual report records the closing and the price in a
subsequent-events note; the former owner's landing page now states that its digital-markets business
is G2's; and the shared community guidelines define the operator as a single group of entities behind
one contact address.

**Consequence for research, and it is the important one: agreement between G2, Capterra, GetApp and
Software Advice is no longer independent corroboration.** Any guidance describing these as separately
owned, or treating them as a cross-check on one another, is out of date. Effective independent
platforms in this category are now G2's group, TrustRadius, and Trustpilot — three, not five.

## How each one is filled

### The G2 group

Vendor-sourced reviews are a documented first-class mechanism, not an abuse. G2 states that it
occasionally offers incentives such as gift cards or a donation for honest reviews, that eligibility is
never based on the opinion expressed, that confirmed incentivized reviews are **tagged as such**, and
that it caps any incentive at **$100** across cash, gift cards, and non-cash items. Every review
passes automated filtering and then manual moderation before publication. A reviewer may qualify by
being on a **seller-provided campaign list**.

What is prohibited is *selection*: collecting reviews by any method that segments out potentially
negative reviews or exclusively targets positive ones. G2 also acknowledges that campaign pages become
targets for people farming the gift cards.

**It sells placement.** Advertising runs on a pay-per-click model as an add-on to paid packages, and
includes placing an ad **directly on a competitor's profile page**; higher tiers offer complete
buyout of ad space. Sponsored profiles on the acquired properties are described by those properties as
paid, with a link-out icon, alongside the statement that all providers are listed and not only paying
ones. The commercial description of the pay-per-click service is explicit that buyers searching on
those sites click a sponsored profile and are directed to a page the vendor chooses.

**A caution about what not to claim:** no G2 page states that paid placement does not affect its score
or grid position. Its scoring documentation does not address the question. Do not attribute such a
statement to it, and do not assert the opposite either — the honest report is that the relationship is
undocumented.

### TrustRadius

The most granular provenance labeling available, and the reason it is worth using. It distinguishes
reviews **independently invited by the platform** from reviews **invited by the platform on behalf of
the vendor**, discloses that both it and vendors use incentives, and states the incentive alongside
the source. Its scoring weights reviews from a random, representative sample **more heavily than
biased samples**. It is also the only platform here explicitly claiming vendors cannot pay to rank
higher, while noting that many review sites openly sell the top spot.

### Trustpilot

The opposite model: an open platform where reviewers need no invitation, invitations are permitted but
must be fair, neutral, and unbiased, and **incentives are flatly banned** — discounts, promo codes,
prize entries, refunds, freebies, or any other benefit connected to the business.

Its own transparency reporting states that **4.5 million fake reviews were removed in 2024,
representing 7% of reviews posted that year**, with 90% caught automatically. That is the only
published base rate of fakery any of these platforms discloses, and it is the number to quote when
someone treats a review corpus as clean. Note the data year: it appears in the report published the
following year, and no later report exists — do not attribute the figure to a more recent year than
2024.

Paid tiers are metered by invitation volume on annual commitments, so review **volume** on this
platform partly reflects spend on invitations.

## The regulation, and the nuance everyone misses

The federal rule on consumer reviews and testimonials prohibits, among other things, writing or
selling reviews that misrepresent that the reviewer exists or used the product; **providing
compensation or incentives conditioned, expressly or by implication, on a review expressing a
particular sentiment, positive or negative**; insider reviews without clear disclosure of the
relationship; operating a review site that falsely presents itself as independent; and suppressing
reviews. It carves out **generalized solicitations to purchasers** — which is exactly why vendor
review campaigns are lawful.

The regulator's own guidance states the nuance directly: the rule does **not** prohibit giving
incentives for reviews, so long as there is no express or implied requirement that the review express
a particular sentiment — while noting that failing to disclose incentives could still violate the
underlying statute. And if a business pays a platform for a benefit, the platform should clearly
disclose the commercial relationship.

Enforcement has landed on exactly the behaviours that corrupt a corpus: suppressing reviews below four
stars while implying the displayed reviews were all of them; managers reviewing their own products
through fake accounts; saturating a platform with tens of thousands of fake positive reviews to dilute
real negative ones; and supplying the means to generate deceptive review content at scale.

## What a review corpus actually establishes

The endorsement rules settle it: an advertiser must possess and rely on adequate substantiation for
claims made through endorsements exactly as if it had made the claim directly, and **consumer
endorsements are not competent and reliable scientific evidence.**

So:

- **A rating average establishes** how a solicited, self-selected population of that platform felt.
- **A rating gap between two products establishes nothing about quality** unless you have established
  that the two populations are comparable — same platform, comparable solicitation programs,
  comparable volume.
- **A high review count may reflect a review-collection program**, an invitation budget, or a gift-card
  campaign rather than enthusiasm. On platforms that meter invitations by paid tier, volume is partly
  a spend signal.
- **The usable content is the text.** Specific named failures, missing capabilities, and comparisons
  to alternatives, counted by distinct reviewer. That is a real finding about a biased sample, which
  is far more than the average is.
- **Check the provenance label before comparing anything.** Where a platform distinguishes
  independently invited from vendor-invited reviews, that distinction is the most important field on
  the page.

## The one thing never to do

Do not build a capability comparison from ratings. Ratings do not say why they were given, may have
been given for reasons unrelated to the attribute in question, and are not competent evidence for an
objective claim. Capability claims come from documentation you read or a product you ran — see
`comparison-discipline.md`.
