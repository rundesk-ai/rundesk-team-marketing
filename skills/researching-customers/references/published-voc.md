# Published customer evidence and how it is selected

Every source in this file was filtered before you saw it. The filter is not random, and knowing its
shape is the difference between using the evidence and being misled by it.

## Reviews: two biases, stacked

Research on the distribution of online reviews names two distinct self-selection mechanisms:

- **Acquisition bias** — mostly people with a favourable predisposition buy the product in the first
  place, so the pool of possible reviewers is already skewed.
- **Underreporting bias** — people with extreme opinions, positive or negative, are likelier to write
  than people with moderate ones.

Together they produce the well-documented **J-shaped distribution**: positively skewed, asymmetric,
bimodal. The consequence stated plainly by the researchers is that the mean rating is a **biased
estimator of product quality.**

The largest study of this compiled more than 280 million reviews across 25 major platforms and found
most reviews on most platforms exhibit a high degree of polarity, with platforms differing by how
selective their customers are about reviewing at all. It confirmed polarity self-selection empirically
and concluded that polarity **reduces the informativeness of online reviews.**

**What a rating average establishes:** how the self-selected extremes on that platform felt.
**What it does not establish:** quality, satisfaction, or how a typical customer experienced the
product.

**Read the text, count the mechanisms.** The usable content in reviews is the specific failure or
delight described — a named step, a missing capability, a comparison to an alternative. Extract those
and count how many distinct reviewers name each. That is a real finding about a biased sample, which
is far more than the star average is.

## The legal layer changes what reviews mean

A federal rule specifically governs consumer reviews and testimonials. It prohibits, among other
things, writing or selling fake reviews that misrepresent that the reviewer exists or used the
product; **providing compensation or incentives conditioned on a review expressing a particular
sentiment, positive or negative**; insiders reviewing their own business without clearly disclosing
the relationship; operating a review site that falsely presents itself as independent; suppressing
reviews by unfounded legal threats or by displaying a subset while implying it is all of them; and
buying or selling fake indicators of social media influence.

Two consequences for research:

1. **Incentivized-sentiment reviews are unlawful, which does not mean they are absent.** A platform
   with vendor-driven review solicitation has a different population than one without. Check how the
   platform says reviews are collected before comparing two products' ratings across platforms.
2. **Generalized solicitation is explicitly allowed.** A business asking all its purchasers to leave a
   review is fine. So a high review count can reflect a review-collection program rather than
   enthusiasm — that is a marketing-operations difference, not a product difference, and treating it
   as signal is a mistake.

Never compare a rating on a platform where the vendor runs a solicitation program with one where it
does not, and never present a rating gap as a quality gap without establishing that the populations
are comparable.

## Forums, communities, and social threads

Participation in online communities is extremely unequal. The widely cited practitioner formulation —
roughly 90% of users read without contributing, 9% contribute occasionally, and 1% account for most
contributions — is not peer-reviewed, and should be cited as the practitioner observation it is. Its
operational point holds regardless: you hear from the same small fraction repeatedly, and they are
almost certainly unlike the large majority you never hear from.

For a company reading community posts as customer feedback, that means the sample is unrepresentative
by construction.

**Use community evidence for:** discovering that a problem exists, learning the vocabulary customers
use, finding the workarounds they build, and spotting a competitor comparison you had not considered.
**Never use it for:** prevalence, priority ranking, or satisfaction levels.

## Support tickets and sales notes

Your own corpus, and the most underused source available — but doubly filtered: only customers who
contacted you, about things they thought you could fix.

- A rising ticket category is a real signal about your own product.
- Absence of tickets is not evidence of absence — people who quietly leave file nothing.
- Sales-call notes record what a salesperson heard and chose to write down, which is a second filter
  with its own incentives.

Where this corpus contains personal data, it is subject to the same handling constraints as any other
customer data; do not move identifiable content into a research artifact.

## What a company says about its customers

Filings, help centres, pricing pages, case studies, and job postings all describe an intended
customer. Treat them as a statement of who a company is **aiming at**, which is genuinely useful —
targeting is a strategic claim — and never as evidence of who actually buys.

## The synthesis rule

When you combine these, keep the provenance attached to each claim, and state the direction of each
bias rather than hoping they cancel. They do not cancel: acquisition bias, underreporting bias, and
participation inequality all point the same way, toward the loud and the committed. A synthesis of
three biased-in-the-same-direction sources is a confident wrong answer.
