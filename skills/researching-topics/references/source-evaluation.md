# Evaluate evidence before using it

Evaluate a source for the exact claim and purpose. A source can be excellent evidence for what an
organization said and weak evidence that the statement is true.

## Read laterally, then vertically

For an unfamiliar online source:

1. **Stop.** Notice the claim, its stakes, and any emotional or confirmatory pull.
2. **Investigate the source.** Leave the page and learn who funds, edits, and vouches for it.
3. **Find better coverage.** Look for sources closer to the evidence and for credible independent
   analysis.
4. **Trace upstream.** Follow quotations, statistics, screenshots, and reporting to their original
   context.

Only then inspect the candidate closely. Professional design, a prestigious domain, and an author bio
are claims the page makes about itself; they are not substitutes for outside context. Controlled
research found that teaching students to leave an unfamiliar page and search laterally improved
their ability to judge digital credibility.

## Test fitness for the claim

Ask:

- **Proximity:** Is this the original evidence, a faithful synthesis, or a retelling?
- **Competence:** Does the producer have relevant expertise, access, and editorial or methodological
  safeguards for this claim?
- **Method:** Are definitions, sample, comparison, measurement, and limitations visible?
- **Currency:** Is the source current for the version, law, market, or practice being discussed?
- **Independence:** Does it add evidence, or repeat a shared press release, dataset, or study?
- **Incentives:** What interests, sponsorship, advocacy, or selection effects may shape it?
- **Integrity:** Is there a correction, retraction, erratum, or later work that changes the result?
- **Applicability:** Does its population, jurisdiction, environment, or workload match the question?

Do not assign a universal credibility score. Authority is constructed and contextual: official
documentation is strong evidence of a documented contract, while independent measurements may be
stronger evidence of real-world performance.

```text
Bad:  "peer reviewed" -> accept every conclusion
Good: inspect the study design, population, measures, limits, corrections, and later evidence

Bad:  "official source" -> treat a marketing benchmark as independent performance proof
Good: use it for the vendor's stated method; seek independent measurements for comparative claims
```

## Handle conflicts and missing evidence

When sources disagree, compare what they actually measured or defined before choosing a winner.
Differences often come from versions, populations, dates, incentives, or methods rather than a simple
true/false split.

- Prefer the source closest to the claim, but keep context and incentives visible.
- Report a credible minority or unresolved result when it could change the decision.
- Do not count mirrors, syndicated stories, or papers analyzing the same dataset as independent
  replications.
- If a key source is inaccessible, do not imply it was verified. Name the limitation and use an
  accessible original or reliable synthesis when possible.
- For research literature, check the current record for corrections and retractions.

Absence of evidence is not automatically evidence of absence. Say what was searched, what was not
found, and whether the search was capable of detecting the thing in question.
