# Segments, personas, and jobs: what each one's standing actually is

These frameworks are useful. They are also cited with a confidence their evidence base does not
support, and a research deliverable that leans on one should say which it is using and what that
buys.

## Segmentation

**Needs and benefit segmentation is the older and better-founded idea.** Classifying buyers by the
benefit they seek rather than by who they are dates to the 1960s marketing literature, and the
non-demographic turn was introduced in the management literature in 1964. The academic distinction
worth carrying is between **a priori segmentation** — you decide the groups in advance from
characteristics you can already observe — and **data-driven segmentation**, where groups emerge from
the data. The first is convenient and often circular; the second requires data most teams do not
have.

**The live critique cuts both ways.** A prominent practitioner reassessment argues that demographic
and psychographic approaches have both become weak predictors of purchasing behaviour. This is
managerial writing rather than peer-reviewed research, and it is worth citing as the informed
practitioner position, not as a finding.

A segment is three separate claims stacked:

1. The group **exists** as a coherent cluster.
2. It **behaves** differently in a way that matters.
3. It can be **reached** distinctly.

Say which of the three you have evidence for. Most segmentations establish the first by construction,
assert the second, and never test the third.

## Personas

The methodological critique here is unusually direct, comes from practitioners inside the field, and
has not been answered:

- Personas **cannot be adequately verified or falsified**, and therefore have no demonstrable
  validity.
- There is **little peer-reviewed discussion** of the method and no literature systematically
  evaluating its methodology or utility.
- The **curse of dimensionality**: as a persona gains features, the share of the population it
  represents falls. The specificity that makes a persona feel real is exactly what makes it
  unrepresentative.
- You cannot assemble a composite description from scattered data points and know its relationship to
  a population. The multivariate correlation data required is expensive, and to the critics'
  knowledge has never been demonstrated for any persona. **Without it, you cannot know whether a
  persona represents a million people or zero.**

Their conclusion: until the methodological issues are resolved, personas should not be treated as a
means to communicate data.

The advocacy literature does not really dispute the mechanics. It characterizes personas as imaginary
persons employed to represent segments of real people, traditionally generated from qualitative data
gathered and analysed by hand.

**Practical position for a research deliverable:** a persona is a communication device for a finding
that already exists elsewhere. Never let one be the finding, never attach a population estimate to
one, and never let a persona's invented details acquire the authority of the data it was built from.

## Jobs-to-be-done

**An influential practitioner framework, not a validated method.** A deliberate search for a
peer-reviewed evaluation, validation, or systematic review of jobs-to-be-done found none. That is a
reportable negative result, and it is why this package uses the lens without citing it as evidence.

What the framework's own institutional home says: it is a lens revealing the circumstances or forces
that drive people toward and away from decisions. Notably, that framing offers no statement of an
evidence base, no origin account, and no methodology — only illustrative cases. **The absence is
itself citable.**

The most famous case study is worth knowing precisely because of what it actually was: a full day
spent observing purchases in one restaurant, followed by interviewing customers on their way out about
what job they had hired the product to do. The number of interviews is not stated. Its finding — that
most buyers used the product for a similar job during a long, boring commute — is a genuine
observation from **an illustrative case, not an experiment.**

The segmentation argument underneath it is the durable part and is worth quoting to a stakeholder:
being in a demographic bracket does not *cause* a purchase; it may correlate with the decision without
causing it.

The related outcome-driven strand argues that companies fail by building the solutions customers ask
for, since customers describe solutions rather than needs. The primary methodological article is
paywalled and unverified here, and the strand's headline success rates are vendor claims. Use the
premise; do not repeat the numbers.

## How to use all three without overclaiming

- Use a **job** to frame what to ask about. It generates good interview questions and bad evidence.
- Use a **segment** when you can name which of the three stacked claims you have tested.
- Use a **persona** only to communicate a finding, and keep the finding's real provenance attached.
- When a stakeholder treats any of them as established method, say what it actually is. That is not
  pedantry — a roadmap built on an unfalsifiable persona is a roadmap nobody can argue with, which is
  the opposite of useful.
